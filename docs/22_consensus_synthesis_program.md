# ARG Consensus Synthesis Program

## Status

> **Candidate convergence synthesis — not a claim of a new fundamental geometry, a completed ontology, or a novel scientific law.**

This program changes the order of operations for Apophatic Relational Geometry (ARG):

$$
\boxed{
\text{research first}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{synthesize second}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{formalize third}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{experiment only on the unresolved remainder}.
}
$$

The purpose is to determine what picture of relation, change, persistence, causation, organization, scale, and representation is already constrained by established mathematics, reproducible science, and disciplined philosophy. ARG may contribute a useful synthesis even when every ingredient is already known. Synthesis is not itself evidence of novelty or truth.

This track is parallel to the existing four-model software and experiment track. It does **not** authorize the blocked development pilot, change a frozen protocol, or promote any model claim.

---

# 1. Governing question

ARG asks:

> **What is the strongest minimal account of structured becoming that survives translation across mathematics, physics, dynamical systems, computer science, biology, and philosophy without exceeding the evidence of any field?**

The goal is not to make every discipline use one vocabulary. Different perspectives may describe different aspects, scales, or inferential roles of the same phenomenon. The research task is to identify:

1. where the fields genuinely agree;
2. where statements are exact translations, specializations, or analogies;
3. where accepted observations coexist with interpretive disagreement;
4. which proposed ARG statements are already established;
5. which statements remain open;
6. whether any precise residual contribution remains after the audit.

---

# 2. What cross-disciplinary consensus means

Consensus is not a vote among disciplines. It is convergence under independent constraints.

A candidate consensus statement must satisfy all applicable tests:

- **mathematical coherence** — definitions are typed, assumptions explicit, and deductions valid;
- **empirical adequacy** — where the claim is empirical, it is supported by reproducible observations or experiments;
- **mechanistic adequacy** — where a mechanism is claimed, interventions or difference-making evidence distinguish it from alternatives;
- **representational robustness** — the claim survives admissible relabelings, coordinate changes, or equivalent reformulations;
- **scope discipline** — conclusions remain within the domain established by the evidence;
- **cross-field translatability** — mappings state what is preserved and where the mapping fails;
- **apophatic restraint** — no successful model term is promoted into an absolute ground.

A field contributes according to its competence:

| Perspective | Primary contribution | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | Possibility, consistency, invariance, equivalence, theorem and counterexample | Which formal structure is physically realized |
| Physics | Quantitative constraints from observation, experiment, symmetry, and successful theory | A unique metaphysical interpretation of all successful formalisms |
| Dynamical systems and control | Stability, attractors, transients, state dependence, intervention, controllability | Ontological fundamentality |
| Computer science and information theory | Representation, computation, state transition, encoding, complexity, recoverability | That reality literally is a computation or data structure |
| Biology and systems science | Organization, function, adaptation, development, multiscale persistence | Fundamental physical ontology |
| Philosophy | Conceptual clarification, inference limits, ontology, explanation, and category-error detection | Replacement of mathematical proof or empirical evidence |

# 3. Evidence and consensus grades

Every substantive statement must be classified by both **claim type** and **consensus grade**.

## Claim types

- mathematical definition;
- mathematical theorem;
- empirical regularity;
- mechanistic claim;
- modeling convention;
- bridge proposition;
- interpretive thesis;
- metaphysical conjecture;
- research question.

## Consensus grades

- **C4 — Established:** textbook-level theorem, definition, or repeatedly replicated scientific result within a clear scope.
- **C3 — Strong convergence:** substantial independent evidence and broad expert agreement, with limited live dispute about the scoped statement.
- **C2 — Shared evidence, disputed interpretation:** the observational or formal core is accepted, but its meaning or ontology remains contested.
- **C1 — Plausible synthesis:** compatible with established results but not itself established.
- **C0 — Speculative:** conjecture or open research question.
- **R — Rejected:** contradicted, disproved, withdrawn, or based on an invalid inference.

The **Minimal ARG Core** may initially contain only C3 and C4 statements. C2 statements belong in the disagreement map. C0 and C1 statements belong in the research agenda, not in the project’s binding ontology.

---

# 4. Required distinction: evidence is not interpretation

ARG must keep four levels separate:

1. **Empirical findings** — observations produced under a stated protocol.
2. **Formal results** — definitions, equations, proofs, mechanisms, and simulations.
3. **Working synthesis** — a cross-field interpretation that organizes established results.
4. **Apophatic restraint** — explicit refusal to treat any representation as exhaustive or absolute.

Allowed inference:

$$
\text{evidence may constrain formal models, and formal models may motivate interpretation}.
$$

Prohibited inference:

$$
\text{successful model}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{final ontology}.
$$

# 6. Research workflow for each topic

Every topic proceeds through the following gates.

## Gate A — Claim decomposition

Convert broad prose into atomic claims. Separate definitions, empirical statements, mathematical facts, mechanisms, and interpretations.

## Gate B — Terminology translation

Map ARG language into the established vocabulary of each field. Record whether a proposed connection is:

- identity;
- exact translation;
- specialization;
- generalization;
- approximation;
- analogy;
- loose resemblance.

Loose resemblance cannot support a bridge proposition.

## Gate C — Primary-source evidence map

For every atomic claim, record:

- original theorem, equation, experiment, or observation;
- assumptions and domain;
- replication or review status;
- known exceptions;
- competing interpretations;
- mapping into ARG terminology.

Primary sources and authoritative reviews take precedence over summaries.

## Gate D — Consensus extraction

State the weakest proposition supported by the strongest convergent evidence. Do not import a preferred metaphysical interpretation into the consensus layer.

## Gate E — Disagreement and boundary map

Record where fields or interpretations diverge. Distinguish:

- factual disagreement;
- model-domain difference;
- scale difference;
- terminological difference;
- underdetermined interpretation;
- genuine contradiction.

## Gate F — Bridge propositions

A bridge proposition must specify:

1. source and destination concepts;
2. assumptions;
3. preserved structure or observables;
4. scope;
5. failure conditions;
6. whether the bridge is exact or approximate.

## Gate G — Invariance and representation audit

Test whether the proposed statement survives admissible re-representations. When a statement is representation-relative, label it as such rather than discarding it or presenting it as universal.

## Gate H — ARG integration

Update, as applicable:

- `08_claim_ledger.md`;
- `09_novelty_matrix.md`;
- `10_literature_landscape.md`;
- `13_alignment_and_claim_ceiling.md`;
- `14_roadmap.md`;
- formal definitions and papers.

## Gate I — Residual decision

Classify the result:

- already established;
- established but perspective-relative;
- shared evidence with disputed interpretation;
- useful synthesis;
- open problem;
- candidate novel contribution;
- rejected.

Only open problems and candidate novel contributions may generate new experiments. Established results should be cited and imported rather than rediscovered.

# 11. Stop rules

Pause or narrow a topic when:

- the proposed principle is already a standard scoped result;
- primary fields use superficially similar words for non-equivalent concepts;
- no exact or defensible approximate bridge can be stated;
- the claim depends entirely on an arbitrary representation choice;
- the literature already answers the proposed experiment;
- the evidence supports several interpretations but no consensus ontology;
- the residual question cannot produce a discriminating theorem, observation, or experiment.

A no-novelty conclusion is a successful result of this program.

---

# 12. Governing maxim

> **Establish what is known. Translate without erasing differences. Preserve disagreement where evidence underdetermines interpretation. Formalize only the invariant core. Experiment only on the unresolved remainder. Reify nothing.**
